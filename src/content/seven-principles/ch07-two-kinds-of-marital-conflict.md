## Summary

Not all marital conflicts are the same. Gottman's research reveals two distinct types: **perpetual problems** and **solvable problems**. About 69% of all marital conflicts are perpetual — they never fully go away. You must identify which type you're dealing with to respond effectively.

The key to *all* conflict resolution is communicating basic acceptance of your partner's personality. You can't accept advice from someone who doesn't understand you. Without that foundation, no technique will work.

## Perpetual Problems

These keep coming back in different forms. Think of them as a dialogue you have with your partner over the years — not a problem to solve but a conversation to keep having. They stem from fundamental differences in personality or values.

Perpetual problems become **gridlocked** when you stop talking about them with humor, affection, or acceptance, and instead talk about them with hurt, frustration, or contempt. The problem itself doesn't change — your ability to discuss it does.

Chapter 10 addresses gridlocked perpetual problems.

## Solvable Problems

These are situational. Even if they feel intense in the moment, they don't carry deeper existential meaning. They can actually be resolved with good communication skills.

Chapter 8 addresses these.

## How to Tell the Difference

Ask yourself:

- **Does it feel like "here we go again"?** → Probably perpetual. It relates to fundamental differences in who you are.
- **Is it about a specific situation?** → Probably solvable. It feels less intense and painful — more like a logistical issue than an identity conflict.

The same *topic* can be either type depending on the couple. Money, sex, housework — any of these can be perpetual for one couple and solvable for another. What matters is whether the conflict taps into something deeper about your personalities and values.

## Seventeen Common Areas of Marital Conflict

Each of these areas contains specific sub-issues. As you review them, notice which ones keep resurfacing (perpetual) and which are situational (solvable).

1. **Work stress** — hours, travel, job satisfaction, work-life balance
2. **Housework** — division of chores, standards of cleanliness, who does what
3. **Money** — spending habits, saving, debt, financial priorities
4. **Children** — parenting styles, discipline, activities, screen time
5. **Sex** — frequency, initiation, satisfaction, affection
6. **In-laws and extended family** — boundaries, visits, loyalties, interference
7. **Jealousy** — trust, outside relationships, possessiveness
8. **Recreation** — how to spend free time, hobbies, individual vs. couple time
9. **Friends** — time with friends, which friends, boundaries
10. **Alcohol and drugs** — use, frequency, impact on the relationship
11. **Religion** — beliefs, practices, raising children in a faith
12. **Trust** — honesty, reliability, keeping promises
13. **Personal habits** — annoying behaviors, lifestyle differences
14. **Communication** — how you talk to each other, listening, stonewalling
15. **Making decisions** — who decides, how decisions get made
16. **Resolving conflict** — how you fight, what happens after a fight
17. **Power** — who has it, who wants it, how it's shared

### How to Discuss

Share your answers with your partner. The goal isn't to rehash the argument — it's to understand the deeper context beneath it. Listen without defending yourself. Acknowledge what your partner shares.

If this exercise doesn't help or leads to more conflict, go back to the fondness and admiration exercises in Chapter 4. You must accept and respect your partner's personality before you can solve problems together.
