## Summary

Even solvable problems can damage your marriage if handled badly. Gottman identifies five skills for resolving them:

1. **Soften your startup**
2. **Learn to make and receive repair attempts**
3. **Soothe yourself and each other**
4. **Compromise**
5. **Be tolerant of each other's faults**

These skills build on each other. You can't compromise if you're flooded, and you can't stop flooding if you never learned to soften your startup.

---

## Skill 1: Soften Your Startup

96% of the time, the outcome of a conversation can be predicted by the first three minutes. If a discussion begins harshly, it will inevitably end on a negative note — even if there are nice moments in between.

Women bring up issues more often, so this skill is especially relevant for wives — but it applies to everyone.

### Rules for a Softened Startup

- **Complain, but don't blame.** Use "I" statements, not "You" statements. "I'm feeling overwhelmed by the dishes" vs. "You never do the dishes."
- **Describe what's happening. Don't evaluate or judge.** Stick to facts. "The garbage is overflowing" vs. "You're so lazy about taking out the trash."
- **Be clear and specific.** Your partner is not a mind-reader. Say exactly what you need.
- **Be polite and appreciative.** Add "please" and acknowledge what your partner does do.
- **Don't store things up.** Address issues as they arise. Stockpiling grievances guarantees a harsh startup.
- **Start with something positive.** "I really appreciate how hard you work. I need to talk about how we split things on weeknights."

### Softened Startup Practice

Rewrite each harsh startup as a softer alternative.

**1.** Your partner's mother visits too often.
- *Harsh:* "I can't stand it when your mother comes over."
- *Your softer version:* ___

**2.** You want your partner to cook more or take you out to eat.
- *Harsh:* "You never take me anywhere. I'm sick of doing all the cooking."
- *Your softer version:* ___

**3.** Your partner socializes too much at parties and you feel left out.
- *Harsh:* "I just know tonight you're gonna be flirting shamelessly again."
- *Your softer version:* ___

**4.** You want your partner to ask for a raise at work.
- *Harsh:* "You're too wimpy to get a raise for your own family."
- *Your softer version:* ___

**Tip:** A good softened startup usually starts with "I feel..." or "I need..." and includes a specific, positive request.

## Skill 2: Learn to Make and Receive Repair Attempts

Repair attempts are efforts to de-escalate tension during conflict. They can be awkward or clumsy — what matters is whether your partner *recognizes and accepts* them.

In happy marriages, repair attempts work even if poorly worded. In distressed marriages, even skillful repairs fall flat because of **negative sentiment override** — your partner interprets everything through a negative lens.

Examples of repair attempts: "Can we take a break?" "I'm sorry, that came out wrong." "Let me try again." "I know this isn't your fault." Even a silly face or a touch on the arm.

## Skill 3: Soothe Yourself and Each Other

When you're **flooded** — heart rate above 100 bpm, adrenaline pumping — you can't listen, think clearly, or respond constructively. You're in fight-or-flight mode. Nothing productive happens from here.

### Flooding Questionnaire

Answer True or False for each statement.

| # | Statement | T/F |
|---|-----------|-----|
| 1 | Our discussions get too heated. | |
| 2 | I have a hard time calming down. | |
| 3 | One of us is going to say something we will regret. | |
| 4 | My partner gets too upset. | |
| 5 | After a fight I want to keep my distance. | |
| 6 | My partner yells unnecessarily. | |
| 7 | I feel overwhelmed by our arguments. | |
| 8 | I can't think straight when my partner gets hostile. | |
| 9 | Why can't we talk more logically? | |
| 10 | My partner's negativity often comes out of nowhere. | |
| 11 | There's often no stopping my partner's temper. | |
| 12 | I feel like running away during our fights. | |
| 13 | Small issues suddenly become big ones. | |
| 14 | I can't calm down very easily during an argument. | |
| 15 | My partner has a long list of unreasonable demands. | |

**Scoring:** 1 point for each True. **6 or above** = flooding is a significant problem for you. **Below 6** = this is an area of strength.

### Self-Soothing Exercise

When you notice you're flooded, take a break of at least 20 minutes (it takes that long for your body to calm down physiologically). During the break:

1. **Sit in a comfortable chair or lie on your back.**
2. **Focus on your breathing.** Deep, slow breaths — not shallow or held. Breathe in through your nose, out through your mouth.
3. **Progressive muscle relaxation.** Tense each muscle group for about 2 seconds, then release:
   - Forehead — raise your eyebrows, then release
   - Jaw — clench, then release
   - Neck — press your head back gently, then release
   - Shoulders — raise them toward your ears, then drop
   - Arms — make fists, tense your arms, then release
   - Back — arch slightly, then release
4. **Let each muscle group feel heavy.** Notice the weight of your body.
5. **Let each muscle group feel warm.** Visualize a calming place — a forest, a lake, a beach. Stay with this image for at least 30 seconds.
6. **Find a personal image that soothes you.** Something specific and meaningful.

### Soothing Each Other — Discussion

Sit down together (not during a fight) and discuss:

1. What makes you feel flooded?
2. How do you typically bring up issues or complaints?
3. Do you store things up? Why?
4. Is there anything I can do that soothes you?
5. Is there anything you can do that soothes me?
6. What signals can we develop for when one of us feels flooded? Can we agree to take breaks?

## Skill 4: Compromise

You can't compromise effectively if you haven't done the first three skills. Compromise requires a softened startup, the ability to make and receive repairs, and a calm physiological state.

The goal is not to get your way. It's to find something you can both live with.

### Finding Common Ground: The Two-Circle Method

1. **Pick a solvable problem** you're currently dealing with.
2. **Each draw two circles** — a smaller one inside a larger one.
3. **Inner circle:** Write down the aspects of this issue you absolutely cannot give in on. These are your core needs.
4. **Outer circle:** Write down the aspects you're flexible about. These are areas where you can yield.
5. **Make your outer circle as large as possible** and your inner circle as small as possible. The more flexible you can be, the more room there is for compromise.
6. **Share your circles with each other.** Then discuss:
   - What do we agree about?
   - What are our common feelings, or the most important feelings here?
   - What common goals can we have?
   - How should we accomplish those goals?

The discussion isn't about winning. It's about understanding what each of you truly can't budge on — and building a solution in the space around those core needs.

---

## Skill 5: Be Tolerant of Each Other's Faults

You cannot change your partner. Compromise won't happen if you believe you can. The final skill is accepting the person you're with — imperfections and all.

This doesn't mean tolerating abuse or ignoring real problems. It means letting go of the fantasy that your partner will become a fundamentally different person if you just push hard enough. Focus on what you appreciate. Accept what you can't change.

1. Why do the first three minutes of a conflict conversation matter so much?
2. What's the difference between a complaint and a criticism?
3. Why do repair attempts fail in distressed marriages, even when they're well-worded?
4. What is flooding, and why does Gottman say you need at least 20 minutes to recover from it?
5. In the two-circle compromise method, why should you try to make your inner circle as small as possible?
6. Think of a current solvable problem. Try the two-circle exercise on paper right now. What did you learn?
