## Summary

Fondness and admiration are the **antidote to contempt** — the single most destructive force in a relationship. If you still have a fundamentally positive view of each other and your marriage, the relationship can be saved. Even when things feel negative right now, if fondness and admiration existed in the past, you can revive them.

This system works as a **buffer.** When stress hits, couples with strong fondness and admiration default to a positive interpretation of each other's behavior — what Gottman calls *positive sentiment override.* "She's late because traffic was bad," not "She doesn't respect my time." Without this buffer, every neutral or ambiguous action gets interpreted through a negative lens.

The test is simple: can you still remember what drew you to this person? Can you name things you genuinely admire? If the answer is yes — even if it takes some work to access those feelings — you have something to build on. If the answer is a flat, unequivocal no, the marriage is in serious trouble.

The good news: fondness and admiration can be strengthened deliberately. It starts with paying attention to what your partner does right instead of cataloging what they do wrong.

## The History and Philosophy of Your Marriage

This is a guided conversation — not a quiz. Sit down together and talk through these prompts. The goal is to reconnect with the story of your relationship and remember what brought you together. Couples who can tell their story with warmth and fondness — even when things are hard — are in a much stronger position than couples who rewrite history negatively.

1. **How did the two of you meet and get together?** What were the circumstances? Was there anything about it that was funny, dramatic, or unexpected?

2. **What were your first impressions of each other?** When did you know there was something there?

3. **What do you remember most about your first date?** What stood out?

4. **What were the highlights of your dating period?** What made it special? What do you look back on fondly?

5. **How did you decide to get married?** Was there a proposal? What was it like? Was it an easy decision or a complicated one?

6. **What memories stand out from your wedding?** The ceremony, the reception, the moments in between?

7. **What was the first year of marriage like?** What adjustments did you make? What surprised you?

8. **What about the transition to parenthood?** (If applicable.) How did it change your relationship? What was hardest? What was best?

9. **Looking back, what moments stand out as really hard?** How did you get through them? What did you learn?

10. **How have you survived difficult times together?** What strengths do you draw on as a couple?

11. **Think of a couple you know who have a really good marriage — and one who doesn't.** What's different about them? What do you want to emulate? What do you want to avoid?

12. **Talk about your parents' marriages.** How were they similar to or different from yours? What did you learn — good or bad — from watching them?

13. **Make a chart of your relationship history.** Draw a timeline together — mark the major turning points, the highs, the lows. Where are you now?

## Seven-Week Course in Fondness and Admiration

This is a daily practice: one thought to hold in your mind, and one small task. It takes just a few minutes a day. The idea is simple — you're deliberately training your attention toward the positive.

### Week 1

| Day | Thought | Task |
|-----|---------|------|
| **Mon** | "I am genuinely fond of my partner." | List one endearing or lovable characteristic of your partner. |
| **Tue** | "I can easily speak of the good times in our marriage." | Pick one good time you've shared and write a sentence about why it was meaningful. |
| **Wed** | "I can easily remember romantic, special times in our marriage." | Pick one romantic or special moment and think about it for a few minutes. Let yourself feel it. |
| **Thu** | "I am physically attracted to my partner." | Think of one physical attribute of your partner that you find appealing. |
| **Fri** | "My partner has specific qualities that make me proud." | Write down one quality you're proud of in your partner. |
| **Sat** | "I feel a genuine sense of 'we' as opposed to 'I' in this marriage." | Think of one activity or interest you both enjoy together. Plan to do it soon. |
| **Sun** | "I am genuinely fond of my partner." | Tell your partner one specific thing you appreciate about them. Say it out loud. |

### Weeks 2–7

The book continues this pattern for six more weeks, each with different daily thoughts and tasks that gradually deepen your practice. The themes expand to include:

- Remembering what first attracted you to your partner
- Recalling times your partner was there for you
- Focusing on qualities you admire in your partner as a parent, friend, or professional
- Noticing what your partner does right *today* — in real time
- Expressing appreciation directly and specifically

**The principle stays the same throughout:** one small, deliberate act of attention each day. You're not trying to overhaul your relationship in a week. You're building a habit of noticing what's good.

## Putting It Into Practice

Contempt doesn't appear overnight — it builds slowly as fondness erodes. The antidote is equally gradual: small, repeated acts of appreciation and respect.

If your scores on the questionnaire were low, don't panic. Start with the seven-week course. It costs you a few minutes a day and works on the simplest mechanism there is: what you pay attention to grows.

If your scores were high, don't coast. Fondness and admiration need maintenance. Keep telling your partner what you value about them. Keep remembering your story with warmth. The buffer only works if you keep feeding it.
